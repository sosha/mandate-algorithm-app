The press briefing room at the AU Commission building was not built for this many people.

Yusuf had been in it twice before — once for a routine briefing on Sahel security coordination, once for a statement on South Sudan that no one had attended except him and a stringer from Xinhua who had fallen asleep. The room held perhaps forty people comfortably. There were seventy now. The television crews were along the back wall. The wire service reporters were in the front row. The freelancers — Yusuf included — were standing along the sides, notebooks in hand, recorders held at arm's length, the particular posture of people who did not have assigned seats and were not going to get one.

Diallo entered from the side door and took the podium. He looked exactly as he had looked in every photograph Yusuf had ever seen of him: the same suit, the same bearing, the same specific exhaustion that was not the exhaustion of a man who had not slept but the exhaustion of a man who had been explaining things to people who were not listening for twenty years. Yusuf had filed a profile of Diallo two years ago for one of the European outlets. He had described him then as "a man who carries his continent's exhaustion the way other politicians carry their ambitions." He had not been proud of the line. It was the kind of line his editors liked.

Diallo read the statement. It was the same statement that had been released to the press corps ninety minutes ago — the embargo motion, the vote, the forty-one to thirteen result, the demand for debt relief commensurate with the value of African raw material exports over the previous decade. Yusuf had read it. Everyone in the room had read it. The statement was not why they were here.

They were here for the questions.

The first three questions were what Yusuf expected. A correspondent from AFP asked about the timeline for the embargo's implementation. A reporter from Al Jazeera asked whether the AU had coordinated the vote with any non-African partners. A journalist from Kenya's Citizen Radio asked what Diallo's message was for African citizens who would be affected by Chinese countermeasures. Diallo answered each one with the practiced fluency of a man who had spent six weeks preparing for these specific questions.

Then the press secretary — a woman in a dark blazer who had been watching the room with the particular vigilance of someone whose job was to end things at the right moment — pointed at the fourth row. A correspondent from Le Monde asked about the legal framework for the embargo under WTO rules.

Yusuf had been waiting. He raised his hand. The press secretary saw him. She hesitated — he was not on the approved list, he was freelance, he was standing along the side wall — and then she pointed at him, possibly because he was the only person in the room whose hand was still up.

"Chairman Diallo," he said. "The military response began within four hours of the vote result being transmitted. Can you tell us what model you believe was used to generate that response?"

The room went quiet.

Not silent — there was the shifting of bodies in chairs, the particular sound of journalists looking at each other. But the questions stopped. The television cameras did not move. The correspondents in the front row turned their heads slightly, the particular angle of people listening to something they had not expected.

The press secretary leaned toward the microphone. Yusuf could see her mouth opening — the beginning of the sentence that would move them to the next question, the procedural deflection that press secretaries used when a question was off-brief or too specific or heading somewhere the briefing was not designed to go.

Diallo raised his hand. Not toward the microphone — toward the press secretary. A small gesture. The hand lowered. The press secretary sat back.

Diallo looked at Yusuf. The look lasted two seconds. In those two seconds, Yusuf understood something about the man at the podium that the profile he had written two years ago had not captured. Diallo was not surprised by the question. He was not caught off guard. He was recognising it — the way a man who has been thinking something privately for weeks recognises the moment when someone else says it aloud.

"That is the question, isn't it," Diallo said.

He said it quietly. The microphone carried it. The translation booths carried it. The room heard it.

Then the press secretary moved on to the next journalist. A correspondent from Bloomberg asked about the effect on commodity prices. The briefing continued. The moment passed the way moments pass in press conferences — absorbed into the record, noted by the people who noted things, missed by the people who didn't.

---

After the briefing, Yusuf went to the press working room on the first floor. He sat at a desk that was not his and opened his notebook. He did not write the story yet. He wrote one line:

*Four hours. What runs in four hours?*

He looked at the line. It was not an answer. It was a question shaped like an answer — the kind of question you wrote when you knew the shape of what you were looking for but not the name. Something had generated a military response to a political event in four hours. Not a human decision-making process. Not an interagency consultation. Not the kind of deliberation that produced military deployments between sovereign nations. Four hours was the time it took a system to receive an input and return an output.

He did not know what system. He did not know whose. He knew the shape of it.

He filed his story — the routine piece, the embargo and the vote and the coalition dynamics, the story his editors wanted. Then he closed his notebook and sat in the press working room for a long time, looking at the one line he had written, and thinking about what kind of thing runs in four hours.
