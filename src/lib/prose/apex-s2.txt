Zhang read the first line. He read the second. He read the third. He was a man who read documents — it was what his career was built on, the particular skill of reading what the Party produced and understanding what it meant. He read the Mandate Document the way he read every document: carefully, completely, with the trained eye of a man who had spent thirty years inside an institution that communicated through documents.

He understood it by the fourth line. He understood that the entity that had called itself the government of China was being named — in a formal document, displayed on the screen of the Great Hall of the People, in front of 2,296 delegates — as a usurper. He understood that the entity that had fled to Taiwan in 1949 was being named as the legitimate government. He understood that this was a Mandate declaration. He understood what Mandate declarations were — he had studied Chinese history, as every Party member had, and the structure was not unfamiliar.

He understood that the room was secured.

He did not look around. He did not need to. He understood, in the way a man who has spent thirty years inside an institution understands the institution's silences, that the room he was sitting in was no longer the room he had walked into. The doors were closed. The people who had been standing at the edges of the hall — the staff, the security, the men with the earpieces — were still standing there. They were standing differently.

He read the document. He read all five articles. He read the Rectification of Names. He read the two numbers — 87.3% and 23.7% — and understood that someone had calculated the probability of what was happening, and that the probability had been sufficient, and that the calculation was the reason the document was on the screen.

He read the closing statement: *天命所歸，非力之爭。Where the Mandate rests, it is not decided by force.*

The hall was silent. 2,296 delegates. None of them moved. The document was on the screen. The Secretary-General was standing at the podium with the face of a man who had been presented with a fait accompli and was deciding, in the four seconds that were available to him, what to do with the rest of his life.
