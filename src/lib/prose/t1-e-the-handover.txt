James Huang signed the last page at 2:47pm on a Thursday in March.

The handover documentation ran to four hundred and twelve pages. He had checked every page. He had walked through every room in the facility — the server halls, the cooling plant, the backup generation building, the lower level with the locked door he had never been given the key to, the corridor with the conduit he had not asked about. Everything was correct. Everything was exactly what he had built. The building was his work, and his work was done.

The facilities coordinator — a woman named Liu who worked for the Ministry and who had been on-site for the past six months handling the transition — was standing at the door of the server room with a clipboard and two signature pages. James signed the first one: facility handover, structural and systems, certified complete. He signed the second: equipment handover, received by operational authority.

The operational authority was waiting by the door.

He was young. Twenty-eight, maybe twenty-nine. He was wearing a signals division uniform — the particular dark green of Taiwanese military intelligence, the insignia that indicated a rank James did not know how to read. He was holding an access card and a tablet and a duffel bag. He looked like someone who had been travelling. He looked tired in the specific way that people looked tired when they had been working on something they were not allowed to talk about and had been doing it for a long time.

James looked at him the way he looked at everyone who moved into the buildings he built — with the particular assessment of a man who has spent twenty years constructing vessels for purposes he is not told. You looked at the person and you tried to match them to the space. You thought: this is the kind of person who will be working in this building. You did not think: this is what they will be working on. That was not your question. The question was the building. The building was done.

They did not speak. The facilities coordinator handled the handover — she presented the documentation, James signed, the young man signed for the equipment. The whole exchange took eleven minutes. They were in the server room together for all of them — the cold air, the hum of the cooling systems, the particular acoustic of a space designed to house machines that made no sound themselves but whose infrastructure made all the sound in the world. James stood at the far end of the room. The young man stood at the near end, near the door. Between them, the servers that James had specified the cooling for, that he had built the power infrastructure for, that he had spent eighteen months constructing the shell of, sat in their racks and did what they did.

James looked at the young man once more. The young man was looking at the servers. He was not looking at James. He was looking at the servers the way a person looks at something they have been waiting to see for a long time — with the particular focus of someone who understands what they are looking at in a way that James did not and would not and had chosen not to.

James had built thirty-one facilities. He knew what this facility was not. He had never let himself finish the thought about what it was. He was not going to finish it now. The building was done. The documentation was signed. The young man was looking at the servers and James was looking at the young man and neither of them was going to say anything because there was nothing to say that either of them was contracted to say.

He picked up his clipboard. He nodded to the facilities coordinator. He walked out of the server room, down the corridor, through the door he had installed eighteen months ago, into the afternoon light of Nantou County in March — the mountains, the valley, the road back to Taichung.

He drove home with the windows down. The air was cool. The mountains were behind him. The facility was behind him. The young man was inside it.

Mei-ling had a school concert that evening. She was playing cello — she had been playing for three years, since she was thirteen, and she was good in the way that children who practised because they wanted to were good, not the way that children who practised because their parents made them were good. He had promised to be there. He would be there. He would sit in the audience and watch her play and think about the music and not about the building.

He would not think about the building. He would not think about the young man. He would not think about the servers or the cooling or the forty-seven megawatts or the conduit behind the cable trays. He would think about the cello.

The building existed. It was correct. It was someone else's now.
