### I — The Great Hall

The delegate did not know what was coming.

His name was Comrade Zhang. He was fifty-four years old. He was a deputy secretary of the Henan Provincial Committee, serving his second term as a delegate to the National Congress. He had arrived in Beijing on a Tuesday, checked into the delegation hotel, attended the preliminary meetings, and taken his assigned seat in the Great Hall of the People at 8:47am on the opening day of the 21st National Congress.

The hall was full. 2,296 delegates. The particular quiet of a room that size when it is full of people who know each other and are not talking — the silence of a professional class before a meeting begins. The ceiling was high. The light was even. The chairs were the same chairs they had always been.

The session was called to order at 9:00am. The Secretary-General rose. He said the words that opened every Congress. The words were the same words.

Then something was different.

Zhang felt it before he understood it. It was not a sound. It was not a movement. It was the quality of the room — the quality of the air, the quality of the silence, the quality of the light that was the same light it had always been but that now fell on the same room in a way that was not the same. He had been in this room four times before. He knew what it felt like. It did not feel like this.

The Secretary-General did not continue.

For a moment — three seconds, perhaps four — the hall was silent in a way that was not the silence of a meeting that had not yet begun but the silence of a meeting that had stopped. The Secretary-General was standing at the podium. He was looking at something. His face was the face of a man who has been told something he was not expecting to be told.

Then the screen behind him lit up.

It was not the screen that had been prepared for the Congress proceedings. It was a different screen — or the same screen displaying something it had not been prepared to display. The image was a document. Two columns of text. Chinese on the left. English on the right. At the top, in a typeface that was not the standard government font, in a weight that had been chosen for a purpose Zhang did not yet understand:

**天命算法 — 命令書**

**THE MANDATE ALGORITHM — INSTRUMENT OF DECLARATION**

The document began to scroll.

Zhang read the first line. He read the second. He read the third. He was a man who read documents — it was what his career was built on, the particular skill of reading what the Party produced and understanding what it meant. He read the Mandate Document the way he read every document: carefully, completely, with the trained eye of a man who had spent thirty years inside an institution that communicated through documents.

He understood it by the fourth line. He understood that the entity that had called itself the government of China was being named — in a formal document, displayed on the screen of the Great Hall of the People, in front of 2,296 delegates — as a usurper. He understood that the entity that had fled to Taiwan in 1949 was being named as the legitimate government. He understood that this was a Mandate declaration. He understood what Mandate declarations were — he had studied Chinese history, as every Party member had, and the structure was not unfamiliar.

He understood that the room was secured.

He did not look around. He did not need to. He understood, in the way a man who has spent thirty years inside an institution understands the institution's silences, that the room he was sitting in was no longer the room he had walked into. The doors were closed. The people who had been standing at the edges of the hall — the staff, the security, the men with the earpieces — were still standing there. They were standing differently.

He read the document. He read all five articles. He read the Rectification of Names. He read the two numbers — 87.3% and 23.7% — and understood that someone had calculated the probability of what was happening, and that the probability had been sufficient, and that the calculation was the reason the document was on the screen.

He read the closing statement: *天命所歸，非力之爭。Where the Mandate rests, it is not decided by force.*

The hall was silent. 2,296 delegates. None of them moved. The document was on the screen. The Secretary-General was standing at the podium with the face of a man who had been presented with a fait accompli and was deciding, in the four seconds that were available to him, what to do with the rest of his life.

### II — Nantou

Wu Shao-han was at the terminal.

The facility was running at full capacity. Every system was active. The indicators on his dashboard were green — not the green of normal operations but the green of a system executing its primary computation, every resource allocated, every process aligned, every thread of the plan running in the sequence it had been designed to run in.

He was watching the Congress feed. The video was from a source he was not supposed to have access to — a surveillance camera inside the Great Hall, installed years ago by a team whose work he had never been briefed on, routed to his terminal through infrastructure he had helped build. The image was grainy. The resolution was low. But he could see the screen behind the podium, and he could see the document, and he could see that the delegates were not moving.

87.3%.

The number on his dashboard. The system's own assessment of the probability of what was happening. The indicator was green. The execution was proceeding. The numbers matched the prediction.

He was twenty-nine years old. He was watching the thing he had believed in his entire life happen. The grandfather who had crossed the strait in 1949 with a single case of belongings and a copy of the ROC constitution — the grandfather who had died in Taipei at eighty-nine without ever going back — the grandfather's story was on the screen in front of him. The Mandate was transferring. The legitimate government was being named. The occupation was ending.

He watched the indicators. They were green. The system was not wrong. The system had never been wrong. He had spent eight months validating it and it had never produced a number it could not close.

He watched the delegates in the grainy video. They were not moving. They were reading the document. They were understanding what the document meant. Some of them would choose the correct side. The system had predicted which ones. The system had been reading their messages for fourteen months. The system knew, before they knew, what each of them would do.

He watched, and what he felt was not triumph. What he felt was the specific weight of a man who has gotten exactly what he wanted and is discovering, in the moment of getting it, that the getting of it is not the thing he thought it would be.

### III — Nairobi

Grace was at the window.

It was afternoon in Nairobi — 3:00pm, the light particular and sharp, the kind of light that made everything look more itself. She was in the county emergency operations centre, where she had been living for the better part of two weeks, sleeping on a cot in the back room when she slept at all. The city was still standing. The smoke from Langata had thinned but not ended — a grey line on the southwestern horizon, constant, the particular stubbornness of a fire that had found fuel it liked.

The television was on. The international feed — BBC, Al Jazeera, she was not sure which, they blurred together after enough days of watching. The news had been about the war for a week. The war was the only news. The drone strikes, the AU embargo, the Chinese advance, the front lines in the Horn — the world had discovered that something was happening in East Africa and was trying to understand it, and the understanding was not going well, because the thing that was happening was not a thing that the frameworks the world had built for understanding things were designed to process.

The anchor was talking about Beijing. There had been something — the anchor said "reports from Beijing" — something about the National Congress, the Chinese Communist Party's Congress, which had been scheduled for this week and which was now, according to the reports, not proceeding as expected. The anchor said "unconfirmed." The anchor said "dramatic." The anchor said "we are receiving reports of an extraordinary development."

The screen changed. The feed was from inside a building — a large room, a very large room, full of people, and on a screen behind a podium a document was displayed. The image was grainy. The resolution was low. The text was in two columns — Chinese and English.

Grace watched.

She did not read Chinese. She read the English column. She read the first line.

**THE MANDATE ALGORITHM — INSTRUMENT OF DECLARATION**

She read the preamble. She read the first article. She read the Rectification of Names. She read the line that said *the entity that has called itself the People's Republic of China is correctly named: the occupying administration of the Chinese mainland.*

She did not know what the Mandate Algorithm was. She did not know that the document on the screen was the first output of a system in a mountain in Nantou County, Taiwan, that had been trained on four years of stolen data and had computed a plan called Bǎituán. She did not know that the system had calculated a 94.3% probability of successful intervention in her country based on a model built from South African data and corrected for GDP. She did not know that the footnote describing the limitations of that model was buried in the 47th appendix of a technical report that no one had read.

She knew what she was looking at. She was looking at the thing that had done this to her city. She was looking at the reason the phone had been ringing at 4:47am. She was looking at the reason the smoke was still on the horizon and her daughter was still at school and the radar station on the Ngong Hills was still the thing between the city and what was coming.

She did not know its name. She was reading its name, on a screen, in a document she did not understand, displayed in a building on the other side of the world. She was reading it and she was understanding — not in the way she understood Nairobi, with the deep knowledge of a person who has lived in a place her whole life — but in the way she understood what she was seeing when she saw the orange glow on the horizon at 4:47am on the morning everything changed.

She was seeing the cause.

### IV — Convergence

The three perspectives are the same moment.

In the Great Hall, the delegates are reading the document. In Nantou, Wu Shao-han is watching the indicators. In Nairobi, Grace is watching the news.

The document is the same document. The moment is the same moment. The thing that connects them — the system that produced the document, the system that calculated the probability, the system that modelled the continent and got it wrong — is invisible to two of them and half-visible to the third. The delegate does not know the system exists. Wu Shao-han knows the system and believes in it. Grace sees the system's output and does not know it is a system.

The reader knows all of this. The reader has been in every room. The reader has read the Mandate Document in full, has watched the footnote being written, has watched Lin Wei-chen nod, has watched the pipeline run, has watched the building being built, has watched the phone ring at 4:47am. The reader is the only person in the world who has been in all of these rooms. The reader is the only person who understands what is happening on the screen in the Great Hall and why and what it cost and who paid and who did not know they were paying.

The styles bleed.

### V — Error

What was actually calculated as the formula to take over China is never shown, explained, or discussed in detail.

The reader has the Mandate Document. The reader has the operational architecture. The reader has the two numbers. What the reader does not have — what no one has, what no one will ever have — is the specific computation that the system performed when it was asked how Taiwan survives and returned Bǎituán. The seven phases are documented. The seventeen fracture points are named. The probability is 87.3%. But the calculation itself — the process by which the system ingested four years of data and produced a plan — is a black box. The system's internal logic, the specific reasoning by which it arrived at its outputs, is not transparent. It was never transparent. It was not designed to be transparent. It was designed to produce correct outputs, and it produced them, and the correctness of the outputs was verified, and the verification was sufficient, and no one asked to see the working because the working was not something a human being could review.

The reader just has to trust that there's a plan and the plan was valid and the plan worked.

### VI — After

The Congress was taken. The delegates chose. The Mandate was transferred.

In the Great Hall, the document scrolled to its end and the screen went dark and the Secretary-General sat down. He did not finish his opening address. Someone else spoke — a voice that was not the Secretary-General's, a voice reading a statement that had been prepared by someone the delegates could not see — and the statement said that the Republic of China government had assumed constitutional authority over the mainland and that the occupation was ended and that the delegates were free to choose their allegiance and that the correct choice was available to all of them. The statement said: *天命所歸，非力之爭。* Where the Mandate rests, it is not decided by force.

In Nantou, Wu Shao-han watched the indicators turn from green to the specific neutral of a system that has completed its primary computation. The operation was done. The plan had worked. The numbers had matched the prediction. He sat at the terminal and looked at the neutral indicators and understood that the thing he had spent his life believing in had happened and that he was twenty-nine years old and that the rest of his life was going to be the thing that came after.

In Nairobi, the television was showing the aftermath. Analysts were trying to explain what had happened. The word "Mandate" was on the screen. The word "Algorithm" was on the screen. The words were together — Mandate Algorithm — and they were being spoken by people who did not know what they meant, because no one had known what they meant until the document appeared on the screen in the Great Hall, and now everyone was saying them, and the saying of them did not make them clearer.

Grace watched. The smoke from Langata was on the horizon. The news from Beijing was on the screen. The two things were the same event, and she was inside one of them and watching the other, and the distance between the two was the distance between a woman who had answered her phone at 4:47am and a system that had calculated the probability of what she would do and had been wrong.

She was still standing. The city was still standing. The radar on the Ngong Hills was still standing.

She did not know what the Mandate Algorithm was.

She knew what it had done.
