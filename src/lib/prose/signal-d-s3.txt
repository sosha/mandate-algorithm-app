He asked his two questions.

The room was quiet. Eleven people around the table, Wu Shao-han at the screen as the technical briefer, and Lin at the head. The screen showed the probability assessment page. Two numbers. Two bars of different heights on a chart that someone had designed to be legible from the back of the room.

Lin said: "What is the probability of successful execution?"

The answer came from the system documentation, read aloud by Wu Shao-han in the flat, precise voice of a young man who had been operating this system for eight months and who understood that the number he was reading was the number the system had computed and that the system did not compute wrong numbers:

"87.3 percent."

Lin said: "What is the probability of Taiwan's survival under the current strategic trajectory, defined as maintenance of de facto independence for a period of twenty years?"

"23.7 percent."

Lin looked at the two numbers. He had spent thirty-seven years calculating odds. He knew what these two numbers meant.

23.7 percent was less than a coin flip. 23.7 percent was the probability that Taiwan survived twenty years if nothing changed. 23.7 percent was the number that said: if you do nothing, you are betting your country's existence on a probability worse than one in four. 23.7 percent was the number that said the thing he had been watching for thirty-seven years — the slow closing of the window, the slow construction of the fait accompli, the slow removal of the American option — was not a future concern. It was a present fact, expressed as a probability, and the probability was 23.7 percent.

87.3 percent was the alternative. 87.3 percent was the probability that the plan worked — that the Congress was taken, that the Mandate was transferred, that the reunification was stable, that the thing he had spent his life calculating the odds of was resolved in Taiwan's favour. 87.3 percent was not certainty. It was close. It was close enough that the gap between 87.3 and 100 was smaller than the gap between 23.7 and 50.

The math was not complicated. 87.3 was better than 23.7. The plan was better than the status quo. The plan was the thing the system had produced when asked how Taiwan survives, and the system did not produce wrong answers, and the answer was Bǎituán, and the probability was 87.3 percent.
