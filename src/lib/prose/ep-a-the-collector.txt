### Scene One — November 2021

The error appeared at 23:14 on a Tuesday.

Chen Hsiu-ying had been staring at the same validation log for forty minutes, working backwards through the pipeline in the methodical way she had learned to work: rule out the obvious causes first, then the less obvious ones, then — if everything else was clean — accept that the problem was upstream and file a query. She had reached the third step.

The error was a schema mismatch. The incoming data from Source Node 7 was structured correctly by every metric she could evaluate: field types matched, null rates were within tolerance, the compression ratio was consistent with the previous forty-two ingestion cycles. But a subset of records was triggering a downstream validation flag she had never seen before. The flag read:

```
RECORD CLASS: UNCLASSIFIED / SOURCE METADATA ABSENT
VALIDATION STATUS: INDETERMINATE
RECOMMENDED ACTION: ESCALATE OR RECLASSIFY
```

She had not written that flag. She had not written that record class. She had built the pipeline's validation layer herself, field by field, over the previous six months, and UNCLASSIFIED / SOURCE METADATA ABSENT was not in her schema documentation. It existed in the codebase somewhere upstream of her access level.

She leaned back in her chair and looked at the ceiling for a moment, then at the two empty coffee cups on the desk, then at the window, which showed her nothing except the reflection of her own apartment: the monitors, the desk lamp, the stacks of printed documentation she kept meaning to file. Zhonghe at midnight was quiet. The building hummed with its own climate systems. Someone in the flat above hers was watching something with a lot of bass.

She pulled up the query form.

---

> **The pipeline has flagged a subset of Source Node 7 records as UNCLASSIFIED / SOURCE METADATA ABSENT. This record class is not in my schema documentation. I am unable to evaluate whether this represents a data integrity issue without understanding the source metadata structure. Can you clarify the record class specification or provide read access to the upstream schema?**
>
> *Chen Hsiu-ying, Data Infrastructure, Contract Reference MEA-2021-447*

She looked at the form for a moment. The query routing options were:

**A — Official channels** (Ministry of Economic Affairs IT governance portal, standard response time 3–5 business days, full audit trail)

**B — Contractor messaging** (direct to the technical liaison assigned to her contract, typical response within hours, no formal record)

---

*[**READER CHOICE A — Official channels:**]*

She used the official portal. She always used the official portal — it was what the contract specified, it created a record, and records were how you protected yourself when something went wrong. She submitted the query at 23:31. Reference number: MEA-IT-2021-7204.

The reply came at 23:47. Sixteen minutes. For the official portal, that was not standard. That was not close to standard. The standard response time was three to five business days.

The reply was two sentences:

> *The UNCLASSIFIED / SOURCE METADATA ABSENT record class is generated by a legacy system integration that predates the current schema documentation. Please classify affected records as acceptable variance and continue. — Technical Liaison, Project Oversight*

She had not been given a name. The reply came from a position title, not a person.

She looked at the two sentences for a moment. Sixteen minutes through the official portal. No name on the reply. A legacy system predating her documentation that she had never been told about.

She classified the records as acceptable variance. She continued.

---

*[**READER CHOICE B — Contractor messaging:**]*

She used the contractor channel. It was faster, it was what everyone used, and she needed the answer tonight. She sent the message at 23:31.

The reply came at 23:47.

> *The UNCLASSIFIED / SOURCE METADATA ABSENT record class is generated by a legacy system integration that predates the current schema documentation. Please classify affected records as acceptable variance and continue. — Technical Liaison, Project Oversight*

No name. A position title. A reply in sixteen minutes at eleven-thirty at night.

She looked at the message for a moment. Then she classified the records as acceptable variance and continued.

There was no paper trail of this exchange. This fact would become relevant later, though not to her.

---

By 1am she had cleared the validation backlog, regenerated the affected batch, and confirmed that the pipeline was running cleanly. She made a note in her working document:

*Source Node 7: occasional UNCLASSIFIED / SOURCE METADATA ABSENT records. Legacy integration. Classify as acceptable variance per Technical Liaison instruction (Nov 2021). No action required.*

She closed her laptop, rinsed the coffee cups, and went to bed. The pipeline ran without her, ingesting, validating, routing. By morning it had processed another four million records.

She did not think about the sixteen-minute reply time again for two years.

---

### Scene Two — March 2023

The pipeline had grown.

This was expected — Hsiu-ying had spec'd it to scale, had built the ingestion layer with headroom for a tenfold increase in throughput, and in the sixteen months since the Source Node 7 anomaly the volume had increased by a factor of six. What she had not anticipated was the shape of the growth.

She was looking at a throughput graph on her second monitor, the one she kept in portrait orientation for logs and charts, while the main screen ran her development environment. The graph showed total daily ingestion volume by source node across the past twelve months. It was a tidy visualisation — clean lines, consistent growth, the kind of output you'd put in a progress report without annotating it because the trend spoke for itself.

Except she was looking at Source Node 7.

The other nodes had grown proportionally — roughly in line with the overall six-times increase. Source Node 7 had grown by a factor of thirty-one. Thirty-one times the ingestion volume it had been running when she first set it up. It was now the largest single source in the pipeline by a margin that made every other node look like a rounding error.

She had not noticed this before because she had not been looking at relative proportions. She had been looking at totals. The totals were fine. The pipeline was performing within spec. Everything she was responsible for was running correctly.

She pulled up the metadata for Source Node 7.

```
NODE: SN-07
DESIGNATION: [RESTRICTED]
DATA CLASS: TECHNICAL / PROPRIETARY — THIRD PARTY
SOURCE LOCATION: [RESTRICTED]
ACCESS LEVEL REQUIRED FOR METADATA: L4-CLEARANCE
CURRENT USER CLEARANCE: L2-STANDARD
```

She had L2 clearance. She had always had L2 clearance. She had not known until this moment that there were fields in the pipeline's own metadata that her clearance level would not let her read.

She thought about filing a query. She thought about the sixteen-minute reply at 23:47 on a Tuesday in November two years ago. She thought about the name that had not been on the reply.

She closed the metadata window and went back to the development environment.

The node was running within spec. The data was structurally consistent. The validation layer she had built was handling the volume correctly. These were the things she was responsible for. The source location, the designation, the third-party data class — these were above her clearance level for a reason, and the reason was that she was a data infrastructure contractor, not an analyst, and the Ministry of Economic Affairs managed its data sources according to its own protocols which were not her concern.

She updated her working document:

*Source Node 7: volume growth 31x over baseline (Jan 2022 – Mar 2023). Within infrastructure spec. Source metadata restricted to L4+ clearance. No action required from infrastructure perspective.*

She filed no query. This too would become relevant later.

---

The work was interesting. This was the thing she returned to, in the moments when she noticed something she might otherwise have pursued. The work was genuinely interesting. The pipeline was one of the most sophisticated data integration systems she had built — the multi-source reconciliation logic alone had taken four months to get right, and she had got it right, and she was proud of that in the quiet way she was proud of things she did well. The contract paid 40% above her previous rate. She had moved from a shared apartment in Xinzhuang to this one, which she had to herself, which had a second bedroom she used as an office.

She worked at night. She had always worked at night. The Ministry liaison called in the mornings when he called at all, which was rarely — the system was running well and there was nothing to discuss. She spent her evenings in the apartment with the monitors and the logs and the occasional anomaly that resolved cleanly once she looked at it properly.

She had not thought about what the pipeline was for since the first month of the contract.

### Scene Three — September 2024

The anomaly was different this time.

She almost missed it. It was embedded in a standard validation summary, buried between two line items that were unremarkable, and she had been moving through the summary quickly because it was late and she had been working since morning and the pipeline had been running cleanly for six weeks.

```
BATCH: SN07-2024-09-14-0300
RECORDS PROCESSED: 4,847,221
VALIDATION STATUS: 4,847,019 PASS / 202 FLAG
FLAG TYPE: TEMPORAL ANOMALY — RECORD TIMESTAMP PREDATES SOURCE INTEGRATION
```

She stopped.

A temporal anomaly. Records with timestamps predating the source integration. That should not be possible. The pipeline's ingestion layer she had built rejected records with timestamps outside a defined window — it was a basic data hygiene rule, one of the first constraints she had written, because historical record contamination was a known failure mode in systems that pulled from multiple sources with inconsistent clock synchronisation.

If records were getting through with timestamps predating the integration, one of two things was true. Either her validation layer had a bug she had missed for three years. Or the records were not anomalous by mistake.

She pulled the flagged records.

They were not a random sample. They were not distributed across the batch the way random validation failures would be — scattered, uncorrelated. They clustered. 198 of the 202 records clustered around four specific time windows in 2019 and 2020, before the pipeline existed. The other four were from 2018.

She looked at the record structure. She could not read the content — the data was compressed and partially encrypted, which was standard for Source Node 7 — but she could read the structural metadata: field counts, record types, compression signatures. She recognised the field structure. She had built the validation rules for it. She had spent two weeks in early 2022 learning the schema so she could build the validation rules correctly.

The 2019 and 2020 records had the same field structure as the current records. Exactly the same. Not approximately the same, not a previous version of the schema — the same. Which meant either the schema had not changed in five years, or the timestamps were wrong, or these records were not from 2019 and 2020. Or — and she stopped on this thought for a moment before moving past it — someone had generated them to look like they were from 2019 and 2020.

She filed a query through the official portal. Query reference: MEA-IT-2024-9847.

The reply came in eleven minutes.

> *The flagged records are the result of a scheduled historical data integration that was initiated last week. This is an authorised process. Please classify as acceptable variance and continue. — Technical Liaison, Project Oversight*

Eleven minutes. No name. Historical data integration. Authorised process.

She sat with the reply for longer than she usually sat with replies.

Then she classified the records as acceptable variance and continued.

She updated her working document and then, before she closed it, she added a line she had not added before:

*Note: response time for queries related to SN-07 anomalies consistently sub-20 minutes regardless of time of day. Standard MEA portal response time is 3–5 business days. Someone is monitoring query submissions related to this node in real time.*

She looked at the line for a moment. Then she saved the document and closed it and made herself a cup of tea and stood at the kitchen window looking at the building across the street, which was dark except for two lit windows on the fourth floor and one on the seventh.

She went to bed at 1am.

### Scene Four — June 2025

She had been building, for the past four months, a private document she did not store on the Ministry server.

She had not decided to build it. It had accumulated. She had started keeping notes in a local text file — offline, on her personal laptop, not the work machine — because she had begun to notice that the working document she kept on the Ministry server had a quality she did not like: it felt observed. This was not a rational feeling. She had no evidence that anyone was reading her working document. But the sub-20-minute response times, the reply-without-a-name, the restricted metadata — these things had accumulated into a background awareness that someone, somewhere, was paying close attention to what she did with Source Node 7, and that the working document on the Ministry server was part of what they were paying attention to.

The local document was not a complaint. It was not a record of wrongdoing. It was the same kind of notes she kept for any complex project — observations, questions, things she had noticed and not yet resolved. What made it different was that she was keeping it off the network.

The document was forty-three lines long.

The forty-third line, written three weeks ago, read:

*The data has fingerprints.*

She had been thinking about this since March, when she had finally allowed herself to look at what she was actually seeing in the Source Node 7 output — not at the validation status, not at the throughput metrics, but at the structural signatures of the data itself, the patterns in what was flagged and what was not, the way the temporal distribution of records had shifted over the past eighteen months.

She was not an analyst. She could not read the compressed, partially encrypted content of the records. But she could read their shapes, and she had been reading data shapes for long enough that she knew what different kinds of data looked like in profile — financial records had one signature, communications metadata had another, technical documentation had another still. She had built enough pipelines to have learned, without being taught, how to distinguish them by feel.

Source Node 7 did not feel like any single data type. It felt like many data types, compressed together, routed through a single node to obscure their diversity. It felt like — and she had sat with this for a week before writing it down — a collection. Someone was collecting. Someone had built, using her pipeline, a collection of data from many sources, and they were routing it all through a single node to avoid the kind of classification she had just done.

The pipeline was not a competitive intelligence system.

She had known this for some time. She had not let herself finish the thought before. She finished it now.

The pipeline was not a competitive intelligence system for the Ministry of Economic Affairs. The Ministry of Economic Affairs was the cover story. The pipeline was something else — something that required the architecture she had built, the throughput she had optimised, the validation layer she had tuned to handle exactly this kind of diverse, high-volume, partially encrypted data stream. Something that required someone monitoring the query system in real time. Something with L4-clearance metadata she could not read.

She sat at her desk for a long time.

Outside, Zhonghe was quiet. The sky had the particular colour of a hot June night in Taipei — not dark, exactly, but deep orange where the city's light caught the haze, fading to something greyish at the top. She could see a slice of it through the gap in the curtains.

She thought about what it meant to file a report.

She thought: report to whom. The Ministry of Economic Affairs IT governance portal. The same system where her queries went, the same system that replied in eleven minutes in the middle of the night with no name on the response.

She thought: report what. That Source Node 7 had a structure consistent with a broad-based intelligence collection operation. That was what she suspected. She did not have evidence of this — she had a shape, a pattern, a professional intuition built from twelve years of working with data at this level. She had a text file with forty-three lines on a laptop that was not connected to the Ministry network.

She thought about the sixteen-minute reply at 23:47 in November 2021, four years ago, the first time she had filed a query about this node.

She thought: they already know what this is. Everyone above my clearance level already knows what this is. The question they are waiting for me to answer is not *what is it* but *what will she do*.

### Scene Five — November 2025

The pipeline had been running for four years.

Hsiu-ying was looking at the throughput summary for the previous quarter: 847 billion records processed, 99.97% validation pass rate, zero infrastructure failures. By any objective metric, it was the best-performing system she had ever built. She had a standing offer from a fintech company in Singapore — a recruiter had reached out three months ago, the second approach this year, the salary was a third more than her current rate — and she had not replied, which surprised her a little when she thought about it. She liked this work. She was good at this work.

She opened the local text file. It had 94 lines now. The last line, written this morning, read:

*The volume increase in the past six months is not consistent with an intelligence collection operation. The growth curve has changed character. It is consistent with a training run.*

She had written that at 7am, before she had properly thought about what it meant. Now it was midnight and she had thought about it for most of the day and she understood what it meant.

Training runs for large-scale machine learning systems had a distinctive throughput signature. She had not worked on ML infrastructure directly, but she had read enough of the technical literature to know the pattern: initial data collection phase, relatively flat throughput; then a sharp increase as the system moved from collection to active training; then the characteristic surge of a system ingesting everything at maximum throughput before a training cycle completed. The curve she was looking at had all three phases. The collection phase was the first three years of the pipeline's operation. The increase was the past eighteen months. The surge was the past six months.

Something was being trained. Something large. Something that had needed four years of data collection before it was ready.

She looked at the monitoring dashboard on her second monitor. The pipeline was running cleanly. 847 billion records. The validation layer she had built was processing them correctly. The compression ratios were optimal. Everything she was responsible for was performing exactly as it should.

She thought about Wu Shao-han, though she did not know his name. She thought about the person on the other end of the query form — the Technical Liaison, Project Oversight, who had never told her their name and who replied in eleven minutes at midnight. She thought about the Ministry of Economic Affairs and L4 clearance and the metadata fields she could not read.

She thought about what a system trained on 847 billion records collected from TSMC's data infrastructure over four years would be capable of.

She did not let herself finish this thought completely. She had learned, over four years, the specific discipline of not finishing certain thoughts. It was a professional skill, the same as any other.

She opened the reporting interface.

She looked at it for a long time.

---

> **What does Hsiu-ying do?**
>
> **A — She files a formal report**, documenting her concerns about the nature of Source Node 7's data and the anomalous query response patterns. She sends it through official channels. Query reference: MEA-IT-2025-11847.
>
> **B — She closes the reporting interface and continues**, updating her working document with the training run observation. The pipeline runs. She does not report. She does not decide not to report — she simply continues, which is what she has always done.

---

*[**READER CHOICE A — She reports:**]*

The reply came in nine minutes.

> *Thank you for your query. After review, we can confirm that the pipeline infrastructure is operating within its authorised parameters. Your contract has been reviewed and extended for a further two years at a rate increase of 15%. Please continue your excellent work. — Technical Liaison, Project Oversight*

No name. Nine minutes. A rate increase and a contract extension.

She looked at the reply for a long time.

She thought: the reply does not say the query is wrong. It says the pipeline is within authorised parameters. It does not deny anything. It does not explain anything. It offers her more money and more work and no information.

She understood now what her role was. She had always understood, in the way you understand things you do not finish thinking about. She was the person who built the architecture. She was the person who ensured the data flowed correctly. She was the person who, when she noticed things, filed queries that were answered in nine minutes with no name on the response. She was the person who continued.

She accepted the contract extension. The rate increase was significant.

She closed the reporting interface and updated her working document:

*Query MEA-IT-2025-11847 filed 23:34. Response received 23:43. No substantive response to concern. Contract extended +2yr, rate +15%. Continuing.*

She did not write what she was continuing. She did not need to.

---

*[**READER CHOICE B — She continues:**]*

She closed the reporting interface.

She updated her working document:

*Training run signature confirmed in throughput data Q3 2025. Nature of system being trained: unknown. Inferring from data sources and volume: large-scale strategic analysis. No action taken.*

She looked at *no action taken* for a moment, then deleted it and replaced it with *continuing*.

This was more accurate. She was not failing to act. She was continuing. She had been continuing for four years. The continuing was not passive — it was a thing she did each day, each night, each time she opened the query interface and closed it again, each time she classified acceptable variance as acceptable variance and updated the working document and made another cup of tea.

She knew what she was building. She had known for some time.

She continued.
