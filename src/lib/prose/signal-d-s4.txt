He thought about the carriers. The Independence and the Nimitz. Fifty warships in the Pacific, the largest deployment since Vietnam. He had been twenty-four years old. He had watched the news in the intelligence office in Taipei and felt the relief — the specific, physical relief of a man who has been told he is not alone. He had spent the next twenty-five years understanding that he would not feel that relief again. That the world had changed. That the carriers would not come back.

He thought about 23.7 percent. He thought about what it meant for his country, his career, his life's work — the thirty-seven years of reading assessments and writing assessments and calculating odds and finding them insufficient. He thought about the fact that he was sixty-one years old and would retire in four years and that the window would close sometime after that, and that the people who came after him would face the number he was facing now, and that the number would be worse.

He did not think about the forty-seventh appendix.

He was not avoiding it. He did not know it was there. He had read the index. The index said non-consequential. He had moved on. He had sixteen other documents to review. He was a careful reader. He was also a human being who had been reading documents for twelve hours and who trusted the review board's designation because the review board's designation was what the system was designed to produce, and the system did not produce wrong designations.

He looked at the screen. The two numbers. The two bars. The chart that someone had designed to be legible from the back of the room.

He nodded.
