Lin Wei-chen had been in rooms like this before.

The Preparatory Office met in a conference room on the third floor of a building in Taipei that did not appear on any public registry of government facilities. The room was not large — twelve seats around a table, a screen at one end, a water dispenser in the corner, the particular anonymous quality of a space designed to be forgettable. He had been in this room forty-three times over four years. He had read every document the Preparatory Office had ever produced. He was the only person in the room who had.

He was sixty-one years old. He had spent thirty-seven years in Taiwanese intelligence. He had been a young analyst during the 1996 Taiwan Strait Crisis, when Chinese missile tests bracketed the island — warheads landing twenty miles off Keelung and twenty-nine miles off Kaohsiung, two ports closed, 150,000 troops on exercise — and the United States sent two carrier battle groups. The USS Independence and the USS Nimitz, plus an amphibious group, roughly fifty warships. The largest Pacific naval deployment since Vietnam.

He had watched those carriers arrive and felt something he would later learn to name: the specific relief of a man who has been told he is not alone. He had also felt something else, which he did not name for another five years: the specific understanding that this would not happen again. That the United States had sent fifty warships in 1996 because it could, and that the world in which it could was ending, and that the next crisis would find Taiwan alone.

He had been right. He had never been proven wrong. The two Chinese officers who were executed in 1999 for revealing that the missile warheads were unarmed had confirmed what he already suspected: the exercises had been a test, the test had been about American response, and the American response had been noted and would not be repeated in a configuration that mattered. China had spent the decades since building the military capacity to ensure it would not need to be repeated. Anti-access. Area denial. The slow, patient construction of a fait accompli that would arrive before any carrier group could.

Lin Wei-chen had spent thirty-seven years watching this happen. He had read the internal assessments. He had written some of them. He knew what the realistic scenarios looked like. He knew the numbers that the public was not allowed to see — the probability assessments, the force ratio analyses, the models that said, in their clinical language, that Taiwan's window was closing. He had spent his entire career calculating the odds of the island's survival and finding them insufficient.

He was not a villain. He was a man who had done the math for thirty-seven years and understood what the math said.

---

The document was called Bǎituán. The Hundred Regiments.

It was two hundred and seven pages. It had been generated by System Twelve and had been reviewed by the technical validation team, the operational planning cell, and the three members of the Preparatory Office who had clearance to read it in full. Lin was the fourth. He was the person who would decide whether it proceeded.

He read it in five hours. He read the preamble. He read the Mandate Document, which was the first output the system had produced and which he had already read separately, three weeks ago, in the Nantou facility, standing behind Wu Shao-han's terminal at 3am, reading it on the screen while Wu Shao-han sat perfectly still in his chair and did not move. He read the operational sequence — the seven phases, the three-year span, the seventeen fracture points, the Congress operation. He read the infrastructure summary. He read the intelligence architecture. He read the delegate modelling section. He read the trade offer packages — the extraordinary, precisely calibrated offers that would be extended to the major powers, each one targeted at a specific political vulnerability. He noted the Radboud University provision — four thousand Dutch students at a university in Nijmegen, targeted at the re-election prospects of the Dutch Prime Minister — and noted it as an example of the system's targeting precision. He read the probability assessments.

He read the appendices. He read the first forty-six.

He did not read the forty-seventh.

The forty-seventh appendix was titled "Data Limitations and Proxy Methodology for the African Economic Module." It was three pages long. It contained a footnote written by an economist named Kuo Ming-che, who was Lin's nephew, who had taught him AI in his living room with a whiteboard and a patience that Lin had not deserved, and who had written a sentence that said *significant variation in political economy structures across sub-Saharan Africa means that extrapolations beyond South Africa's immediate peer group carry substantial uncertainty at country level.*

Lin did not read this sentence. The appendix was listed in the document's index as *non-consequential to primary objective.* The African chapter of the operational plan was listed as *non-consequential to plan success.* The review board had endorsed the methodology. The documentation standard had been met. The appendix existed because documentation standards required that all known limitations be noted, and Lin had sixteen other documents to review that day, and the appendix was three pages long in a two-hundred-and-seven-page document, and it was in the index, and the index said non-consequential.

Lin Wei-chen was one of the most careful readers in the Taiwanese national security apparatus. He had been described, in a performance review fifteen years ago, as a man who read footnotes. He had read the forty-sixth appendix. He had read the forty-fifth. He would have read the forty-seventh if the index had said consequential. It said non-consequential. He moved on.

He did not know that his nephew had written it. He did not know that the footnote described a model that would produce a 94.3% probability of successful military intervention in Africa. He did not know that the model was wrong. He did not know that the model was wrong because it had been trained on a single country's data and extrapolated across a continent, and that the extrapolation was a guess, and that the guess was documented, and that the documentation said *substantial uncertainty at country level,* and that no one would read the documentation, and that the war the model would produce would reach a city called Nairobi where a woman named Grace Wanjiku Muthoni would answer her phone at 4:47am.

He did not know any of this. He knew the two numbers.

---

He asked his two questions.

The room was quiet. Eleven people around the table, Wu Shao-han at the screen as the technical briefer, and Lin at the head. The screen showed the probability assessment page. Two numbers. Two bars of different heights on a chart that someone had designed to be legible from the back of the room.

Lin said: "What is the probability of successful execution?"

The answer came from the system documentation, read aloud by Wu Shao-han in the flat, precise voice of a young man who had been operating this system for eight months and who understood that the number he was reading was the number the system had computed and that the system did not compute wrong numbers:

"87.3 percent."

Lin said: "What is the probability of Taiwan's survival under the current strategic trajectory, defined as maintenance of de facto independence for a period of twenty years?"

"23.7 percent."

Lin looked at the two numbers. He had spent thirty-seven years calculating odds. He knew what these two numbers meant.

23.7 percent was less than a coin flip. 23.7 percent was the probability that Taiwan survived twenty years if nothing changed. 23.7 percent was the number that said: if you do nothing, you are betting your country's existence on a probability worse than one in four. 23.7 percent was the number that said the thing he had been watching for thirty-seven years — the slow closing of the window, the slow construction of the fait accompli, the slow removal of the American option — was not a future concern. It was a present fact, expressed as a probability, and the probability was 23.7 percent.

87.3 percent was the alternative. 87.3 percent was the probability that the plan worked — that the Congress was taken, that the Mandate was transferred, that the reunification was stable, that the thing he had spent his life calculating the odds of was resolved in Taiwan's favour. 87.3 percent was not certainty. It was close. It was close enough that the gap between 87.3 and 100 was smaller than the gap between 23.7 and 50.

The math was not complicated. 87.3 was better than 23.7. The plan was better than the status quo. The plan was the thing the system had produced when asked how Taiwan survives, and the system did not produce wrong answers, and the answer was Bǎituán, and the probability was 87.3 percent.

He thought about the carriers. The Independence and the Nimitz. Fifty warships in the Pacific, the largest deployment since Vietnam. He had been twenty-four years old. He had watched the news in the intelligence office in Taipei and felt the relief — the specific, physical relief of a man who has been told he is not alone. He had spent the next twenty-five years understanding that he would not feel that relief again. That the world had changed. That the carriers would not come back.

He thought about 23.7 percent. He thought about what it meant for his country, his career, his life's work — the thirty-seven years of reading assessments and writing assessments and calculating odds and finding them insufficient. He thought about the fact that he was sixty-one years old and would retire in four years and that the window would close sometime after that, and that the people who came after him would face the number he was facing now, and that the number would be worse.

He did not think about the forty-seventh appendix.

He was not avoiding it. He did not know it was there. He had read the index. The index said non-consequential. He had moved on. He had sixteen other documents to review. He was a careful reader. He was also a human being who had been reading documents for twelve hours and who trusted the review board's designation because the review board's designation was what the system was designed to produce, and the system did not produce wrong designations.

He looked at the screen. The two numbers. The two bars. The chart that someone had designed to be legible from the back of the room.

He nodded.

---

The room moved. The eleven people around the table — the particular micro-movement of people in a meeting that has just ended, the collective exhale, the reaching for phones. Wu Shao-han gathered his materials at the screen. The figure at the head of the table — Lin Wei-chen, sixty-one years old, thirty-seven years in intelligence, the only person in the room who had read every document the Preparatory Office had ever produced except for one — stood up and walked toward the door.

He did not pause. He did not look back. He walked through the door into the corridor, which was empty, and he turned left toward the lift, and he pressed the button, and he waited.

The lift arrived. The doors closed. He was alone.

He pressed the button for the ground floor. The lift moved. He stood in the metal box and looked at his reflection in the brushed steel doors and thought: 87.3 percent.

He did not think: I have just approved the operational plan that will transfer the Mandate of Heaven from the People's Republic of China to the Republic of China by means of a coordinated multi-phase operation involving data theft, hardware acquisition, delegate surveillance, and the issuance of a document modelled on classical Chinese dynastic proclamations by an artificial intelligence system I helped build.

He thought: 87.3 percent.

The lift reached the ground floor. The doors opened. He walked out of the building into the Taipei afternoon, which was hot and humid and smelled of exhaust and the particular sweetness of the betel nut stalls on the corner. He lit a cigarette. He had quit seven years ago. He smoked it anyway.

87.3 percent.

The number was sufficient. The decision was made. The plan would proceed.

He did not think about Africa.
