He sat at the kitchen table for a long time.

He did not know what the facility was built for. He had never been told and had never asked. But he knew what forty-seven megawatts of installed capacity meant, and he knew the facility had been operational for three years, and he knew that the news was showing him drone strikes on military infrastructure coordinated at a scale that required something — processing, targeting, planning — far beyond what any human team could produce overnight. He knew what the conduit behind the cable trays meant. He knew what Director Chen's signatures had meant. He had always known, in the way you knew things you had decided not to finish knowing.

He folded the acceptance letter carefully and put it back in its envelope.

He called Mei-ling down. They ate dinner. He did not say anything. There was no one to say it to, and nothing that saying it would change. The building existed. The letter existed. His daughter was going to Edinburgh. These three things were all true simultaneously, and he was a man who had spent twenty years building things precisely and correctly to the highest standard he could achieve, and what had been put inside them had never been his responsibility, and he was not going to make it his responsibility now.

He washed the dishes after dinner. He turned off the lights. He went to bed.

He did not sleep for a long time.
