### III — Nairobi

Grace was at the window.

It was afternoon in Nairobi — 3:00pm, the light particular and sharp, the kind of light that made everything look more itself. She was in the county emergency operations centre, where she had been living for the better part of two weeks, sleeping on a cot in the back room when she slept at all. The city was still standing. The smoke from Langata had thinned but not ended — a grey line on the southwestern horizon, constant, the particular stubbornness of a fire that had found fuel it liked.

The television was on. The international feed — BBC, Al Jazeera, she was not sure which, they blurred together after enough days of watching. The news had been about the war for a week. The war was the only news. The drone strikes, the AU embargo, the Chinese advance, the front lines in the Horn — the world had discovered that something was happening in East Africa and was trying to understand it, and the understanding was not going well, because the thing that was happening was not a thing that the frameworks the world had built for understanding things were designed to process.

The anchor was talking about Beijing. There had been something — the anchor said "reports from Beijing" — something about the National Congress, the Chinese Communist Party's Congress, which had been scheduled for this week and which was now, according to the reports, not proceeding as expected. The anchor said "unconfirmed." The anchor said "dramatic." The anchor said "we are receiving reports of an extraordinary development."

The screen changed. The feed was from inside a building — a large room, a very large room, full of people, and on a screen behind a podium a document was displayed. The image was grainy. The resolution was low. The text was in two columns — Chinese and English.

Grace watched.

She did not read Chinese. She read the English column. She read the first line.

**THE MANDATE ALGORITHM — INSTRUMENT OF DECLARATION**

She read the preamble. She read the first article. She read the Rectification of Names. She read the line that said *the entity that has called itself the People's Republic of China is correctly named: the occupying administration of the Chinese mainland.*

She did not know what the Mandate Algorithm was. She did not know that the document on the screen was the first output of a system in a mountain in Nantou County, Taiwan, that had been trained on four years of stolen data and had computed a plan called Bǎituán. She did not know that the system had calculated a 94.3% probability of successful intervention in her country based on a model built from South African data and corrected for GDP. She did not know that the footnote describing the limitations of that model was buried in the 47th appendix of a technical report that no one had read.

She knew what she was looking at. She was looking at the thing that had done this to her city. She was looking at the reason the phone had been ringing at 4:47am. She was looking at the reason the smoke was still on the horizon and her daughter was still at school and the radar station on the Ngong Hills was still the thing between the city and what was coming.

She did not know its name. She was reading its name, on a screen, in a document she did not understand, displayed in a building on the other side of the world.

She was seeing the cause.
