At 2:31pm, someone asked about the South African proxy.

The question came from a man at the far end of the table — someone from the project oversight team, whose name she had not caught. The question was: "Can you explain the decision to use South Africa as the representative model for the continent, and how the correction factors address country-level variation?"

The presenter — Kuo, she thought, Dr. Kuo — nodded. He advanced to a slide she had seen briefly in the handout: a methodology diagram showing the South African baseline model, the correction factor layers, the country-level extrapolation. He explained it the way he had explained everything: clearly, completely, with the specific detail of someone who had thought about this a lot and had concluded that the methodology was defensible.

He used a phrase she had not heard before. He said: "It is a professionally defensible approximation."

Professionally defensible approximation.

She heard the phrase and something in her — not her mind, exactly, but something deeper, the place where professional instinct lived — moved. She had spent three years building a pipeline she was told fed a competitive intelligence system. She had filed 7,204 queries and received replies without names in under twenty minutes. She had classified things as acceptable variance and continued. She had never asked what the data was used for. She had never asked where it went. She had done her work carefully and documented it completely and had not looked sideways because looking sideways was not part of the specification.

*Professionally defensible approximation.* That was what she was doing. That was what they were all doing in this room — the presenter, the oversight people, the contractors, the data infrastructure representative. They were all making professionally defensible approximations. They were all doing careful work within defined scope and not asking what the work connected to, because the work was the work and the scope was the scope and the methodology was documented and the documentation was the floor.

She did not ask where the data originated. She did not ask what the pipeline fed. She sat in the room and listened to a man explain his methodology and thought: yes, that is what we are all doing here, and the thought was not a judgment. It was a recognition. She recognised him. He was someone like her.
