He pressed send at 11:47pm Addis time.

The file was 9,214 words. It had four attachments: the targeting data the KDF signals officer had given him, a timeline of six engagements with their precision patterns, a photograph he had taken himself of a strike site near Garissa where the munition had hit a command tent with no collateral damage to the structures either side, and a 340-word summary in plain language that said what the evidence showed and did not hedge on what the evidence showed. He addressed it to all four outlets. He wrote the cover note in three sentences. He pressed send.

He closed his laptop. The hotel room in Addis was quiet — the particular quiet of a city at nearly midnight, the air conditioning running, the occasional sound of a vehicle on the street below. He had been awake for twenty-two hours. He sat at the desk for a few minutes without moving. He had written what he had seen. He had attached the evidence. He had asked them to publish it. The rest was not his.

The story would not be published until after the apex. This was not his choice and he would not know it was happening. The story arrived at four editorial desks at a moment when the news cycle was entirely dominated by what was occurring in Beijing — the Congress, the document on the screen, the name that everyone was suddenly saying — and the story about the machine running the war was buried under the noise of the larger event it had always been a part of. By the time the editors surfaced it, the apex had already occurred and the story was context rather than news. Yusuf did not know this. He had done what he could do. He had written what he had seen.

The rest was not his.
