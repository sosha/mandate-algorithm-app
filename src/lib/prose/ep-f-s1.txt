Diallo had moved the vote to the emergency protocol chamber.

This was not protocol. The emergency protocol chamber was a windowless room on the second floor of the AU Commission building — smaller than the main Assembly hall by a factor of ten, designed for the Peace and Security Council's fifteen-member sessions, not for a full Assembly vote. It held eighty seats in a horseshoe arrangement. There were fifty-four delegations in the building. Some of them were standing.

He had moved the vote here for a reason he had not shared with anyone except his Chief of Staff, and his Chief of Staff had not asked why because his Chief of Staff had been with him for eleven years and knew when not to ask.

The main Assembly hall — the hall with the two hundred seats and the simultaneous interpretation booths and the polished hardwood and the twenty-story tower visible through the glass wall — was a room China had built. China had built it in 2012, paid $200 million for it, and the servers in its basement had copied data nightly to Shanghai for five years before anyone found out. The servers had been replaced. The encryption had been upgraded. A new Huawei IT cooperation memorandum had been signed two years later anyway, because there was no one else to sign with, and the building still stood, and the tower was still ninety-nine point nine metres tall, encoding the date of the Sirte Declaration in its height, and the room where African leaders negotiated about China was still a room built by China.

Diallo had moved the vote to the emergency protocol chamber because he did not want to hold this vote in that room. This was not a rational decision. The emergency protocol chamber had its own infrastructure, its own interpretation system, its own comms — all of it installed after the 2017 discovery, none of it Chinese-supplied. Whether this made it more secure was a question he did not have time to evaluate. It made him feel differently about standing in it, and that was enough.
