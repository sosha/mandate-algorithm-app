Diallo had moved the vote to the emergency protocol chamber.

This was not protocol. The emergency protocol chamber was a windowless room on the second floor of the AU Commission building — smaller than the main Assembly hall by a factor of ten, designed for the Peace and Security Council's fifteen-member sessions, not for a full Assembly vote. It held eighty seats in a horseshoe arrangement. There were fifty-four delegations in the building. Some of them were standing.

He had moved the vote here for a reason he had not shared with anyone except his Chief of Staff, and his Chief of Staff had not asked why because his Chief of Staff had been with him for eleven years and knew when not to ask.

The main Assembly hall — the hall with the two hundred seats and the simultaneous interpretation booths and the polished hardwood and the twenty-story tower visible through the glass wall — was a room China had built. China had built it in 2012, paid $200 million for it, and the servers in its basement had copied data nightly to Shanghai for five years before anyone found out. The servers had been replaced. The encryption had been upgraded. A new Huawei IT cooperation memorandum had been signed two years later anyway, because there was no one else to sign with, and the building still stood, and the tower was still ninety-nine point nine metres tall, encoding the date of the Sirte Declaration in its height, and the room where African leaders negotiated about China was still a room built by China.

Diallo had moved the vote to the emergency protocol chamber because he did not want to hold this vote in that room. This was not a rational decision. The emergency protocol chamber had its own infrastructure, its own interpretation system, its own comms — all of it installed after the 2017 discovery, none of it Chinese-supplied. Whether this made it more secure was a question he did not have time to evaluate. It made him feel differently about standing in it, and that was enough.

---

The chamber was louder than he had ever heard it.

Not louder in decibels — louder in the quality of the sound. The Assembly hall, when it was full, produced a particular kind of noise: the murmur of diplomacy, of conversations calibrated to be overheard, of languages switching mid-sentence as delegates moved between English and French and Arabic and Swahili and Portuguese. The emergency protocol chamber, with fifty-four delegations compressed into eighty seats, with no glass wall and no two-story ceiling to absorb the sound, produced something rawer. Delegates were talking over each other. Aides were passing notes. The South African delegation — led by the Minister of International Relations and Cooperation, who Diallo had spent three weeks persuading — was in deep conversation with the Zambian delegation, whose Finance Minister had flown in overnight from a restructuring negotiation in Lusaka.

Diallo sat at the front of the room at a table that was slightly too small for the gavel and the microphone and the water glass and the voting tablet and the four sheets of paper in front of him. He was fifty-eight years old. He had been the Finance Minister of Guinea. He had been the AU Commission Chairman for three years. He had spent twenty years before that explaining African economic interests to people who had already decided not to listen, and the particular quality of his exhaustion was not the exhaustion of a man who was tired but the exhaustion of a man who had been patient for longer than patience should reasonably be expected to last.

He had the gavel. He had the numbers. He had spent six weeks building this coalition, and he knew — because he had counted them three times, because his Chief of Staff had counted them twice more, because the-whip-he-had-not-officially-appointed had confirmed them that morning at breakfast — that he had forty-one votes.

Forty-one out of fifty-four. Fifty-five member states, fifty-four present — one abstention, no suspensions. A two-thirds majority of the full Assembly was thirty-seven. He had forty-one, which was four more than he needed, which was the margin he had built because he knew that in a room like this, in a vote like this, three of his forty-one would find a reason to waver in the final hour.

He had built this coalition one country at a time. He knew what each of the forty-one had needed to hear.

---

The Kenyans had needed to hear about the Mombasa-Nairobi Standard Gauge Railway. The SGR had been the flagship project of the Belt and Road in East Africa — $3.8 billion, 90% Chinese-financed, built by the China Road and Bridge Corporation. The loan terms were concessional but the collateral clause had been quietly inserted: in the event of default, the port of Mombasa's revenue stream would be redirected. This clause had not been publicly discussed during the railway's construction. It had been discovered by a parliamentary committee in 2021 and contested in the 2022 election, and the government that won that election had done so partly on the promise to renegotiate. They had not renegotiated. The railway was running. The debt was accruing. The port of Mombasa was, in the technical language of the collateral clause, a Chinese-adjacent revenue facility.

The Kenyan delegation had needed to hear that the embargo motion included a specific provision: debt restructuring would be negotiated bilaterally, with each country's specific collateral exposure taken into account. This had been enough.

The Zambians had needed to hear about the Common Framework. Zambia had been the first African country to default in the COVID era. Their restructuring had taken three years — three years of stalled negotiations in the G20 Common Framework, where China had used the "comparability of treatment" argument to insist that private Western bondholders accept equivalent haircuts before China would move. The Western bondholders had no obligation to do so. The Zambians had spent three years in a trap that was structurally designed to delay relief while interest accrued. The Zambian Finance Minister, who was in the room now, had spent those three years watching his country's fiscal space collapse while a procedural argument played out in conference rooms he was not invited to.

The Zambians had needed to hear that the embargo motion was not a request for charity. It was a demand for structural renegotiation — debt relief commensurate with the value of raw material exports to China over the previous decade. The AidData numbers were clear: Africa had been a net exporter of value to China for twenty years. The debt was real but the trade relationship was not balanced, and the imbalance was the structural fact that the motion was built on.

The Angolans had been the hardest. Angola's debt to China was oil-backed — repayments deducted automatically from a Chinese-managed escrow account before the borrowing government ever saw the money. The Angolan delegation had not wanted to vote for an embargo that would interrupt the flow of oil that was already committed to debt service. Diallo had spent four hours with the Angolan Oil Minister in a hotel suite in Addis, and what he had said to him was this: the escrow model was a model that assumed the flow of oil would continue indefinitely and that the terms of the debt would remain unchanged. Both assumptions were now in question. The embargo was not a rejection of the debt — it was a demand for the terms of the debt to be renegotiated under conditions where Africa held leverage it had not held before. The Angolans had voted yes at 10am that morning.

Thirteen delegations had voted no. Diallo knew which ones and he understood why. Six were China's closest economic partners — Djibouti, where the Chinese naval base sat; Ethiopia, where the Addis-Djibouti railway was the country's economic spine; and four others whose debt exposure or strategic dependency made the embargo an existential risk rather than a negotiating tactic. The other seven had voted no because they were not convinced the embargo would work, or because they feared the military response they could not name but could feel the shape of, or because they had been told — by diplomats, by intelligence services, by the particular architecture of influence that operated in every African capital — that voting yes would have consequences they could not afford.

He did not blame any of them. He had expected thirteen. He had hoped for eleven. Thirteen was within the margin.

---

The vote was conducted by electronic tablet. Each delegation had a device at their seat. The question on the screen was in five languages. The question was:

*Should the African Union Assembly, in extraordinary session, demand debt relief commensurate with the value of African raw material exports to the People's Republic of China over the period 2014–2024, as a precondition for the resumption of normal trade relations, and in the absence of such relief, implement a coordinated export embargo on all primary commodities to the People's Republic of China and its commercial proxies?*

Diallo watched the count on his screen. The numbers moved in real time. Thirty. Thirty-five. Thirty-eight.

Forty-one.

The chamber did not erupt. It did not go quiet either. It did something between the two — a shift in the quality of the sound, a collective intake of breath across eighty seats, the sound of a room that had been holding itself together and was now deciding what to do with what it had just done.

Diallo brought the gavel down. The sound was sharp and clean in the small room.

"The motion is carried. Forty-one to thirteen."

He said it in English. The interpretation system carried it into French, Arabic, Portuguese, Swahili. The delay was three seconds. In those three seconds, before the translations arrived in the earpieces of the delegates who needed them, the room was silent in a way that was not peace but the specific silence of people who understand that a line has been crossed and that lines, once crossed, cannot be uncrossed from this side.

---

> **What does Diallo do now?**
>
> **A — He immediately issues the formal demand to Beijing.** The motion is carried. The AU's legal framework requires that the terms of the demand be communicated to the affected party within twelve hours of the vote. Diallo drafts the communiqué himself. He sends it through the AU's official diplomatic channels — encrypted, formal, addressed to the Ministry of Foreign Affairs of the People's Republic of China, with copies to the United Nations Secretariat and the G20 coordinators. The clock starts now. China has the demand. The embargo trigger is set. The response will come in four hours.
>
> **B — He waits.** The motion is carried but the terms of the demand require further negotiation — which countries' debt profiles are included, what valuation method is used for the "commensurate" calculation, whether the embargo applies to Chinese commercial proxies or only to state entities. He instructs the AU Commission's legal team to finalise the communiqué language within forty-eight hours. This gives China time to respond diplomatically — to offer a counterproposal, to open a negotiation channel, to do the thing that great powers do when they are given an off-ramp. It also gives China time to do something else. Diallo knows this. He chooses it anyway.

---

Diallo stood at the front of the chamber for a moment after the vote was announced. The room was moving — delegates rising, phones being picked up, the particular controlled urgency of people who needed to call their capitals and report what had happened before the news services did.

His Chief of Staff was at his side. She had the phone in her hand — the secure line to the AU Commission's communications office. She was waiting for his instruction.

He thought about the room they were standing in. Not the Assembly hall. The emergency protocol chamber. The small one. The one without the glass wall and the polished hardwood and the tower visible through the window. The one whose servers did not copy data to Shanghai.

He thought about what would happen next. He knew — because he was fifty-eight years old and had been in rooms like this before — that the vote was the beginning of the problem, not the end of it. He knew that the demand, once sent, would produce a response, and that the response would not be what the models predicted because the models had never been built for this. He did not know what the models had been built for. He did not know that a system in a mountain in Nantou County would receive the vote result and generate an assessment in four hours. He did not know that the assessment would calculate a 94.3% probability of successful intervention. He did not know that the basis for that probability was a single country's data, corrected for GDP, filed in a footnote that no one had read.

He knew that he had won a vote. He knew that the winning was the easy part. He knew that what came next was the part that would matter, and that he would not be able to control it, and that no one in this room would be able to control it, and that the room they were standing in — the room China had not built — was the room where they had made the decision that would test whether the room they lived in was the room they thought it was.

He looked at his Chief of Staff.

"Send it," he said.
