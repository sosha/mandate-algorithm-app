Lin Wei-chen had been in rooms like this before.

The Preparatory Office met in a conference room on the third floor of a building in Taipei that did not appear on any public registry of government facilities. The room was not large — twelve seats around a table, a screen at one end, a water dispenser in the corner, the particular anonymous quality of a space designed to be forgettable. He had been in this room forty-three times over four years. He had read every document the Preparatory Office had ever produced. He was the only person in the room who had.

He was sixty-one years old. He had spent thirty-seven years in Taiwanese intelligence. He had been a young analyst during the 1996 Taiwan Strait Crisis, when Chinese missile tests bracketed the island — warheads landing twenty miles off Keelung and twenty-nine miles off Kaohsiung, two ports closed, 150,000 troops on exercise — and the United States sent two carrier battle groups. The USS Independence and the USS Nimitz, plus an amphibious group, roughly fifty warships. The largest Pacific naval deployment since Vietnam.

He had watched those carriers arrive and felt something he would later learn to name: the specific relief of a man who has been told he is not alone. He had also felt something else, which he did not name for another five years: the specific understanding that this would not happen again. That the United States had sent fifty warships in 1996 because it could, and that the world in which it could was ending, and that the next crisis would find Taiwan alone.

He had been right. He had never been proven wrong. The two Chinese officers who were executed in 1999 for revealing that the missile warheads were unarmed had confirmed what he already suspected: the exercises had been a test, the test had been about American response, and the American response had been noted and would not be repeated in a configuration that mattered. China had spent the decades since building the military capacity to ensure it would not need to be repeated. Anti-access. Area denial. The slow, patient construction of a fait accompli that would arrive before any carrier group could.

Lin Wei-chen had spent thirty-seven years watching this happen. He had read the internal assessments. He had written some of them. He knew what the realistic scenarios looked like. He knew the numbers that the public was not allowed to see — the probability assessments, the force ratio analyses, the models that said, in their clinical language, that Taiwan's window was closing. He had spent his entire career calculating the odds of the island's survival and finding them insufficient.

He was not a villain. He was a man who had done the math for thirty-seven years and understood what the math said.
