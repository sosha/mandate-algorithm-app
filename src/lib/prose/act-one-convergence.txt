The corridor on the third floor of the building smelled of climate-controlled air and carpet adhesive. It was the smell of every Ministry building Hsiu-ying had ever been in — the specific antiseptic neutrality of a space designed to be interchangeable, to be any corridor in any government facility in any city.

She was not supposed to be here.

She had come to submit a form in person — a contract amendment, the kind that required a physical signature from someone in Procurement, and Procurement was on the second floor. She had taken the wrong lift and arrived on the third floor before she noticed, and when she turned to go back she saw, through the glass panel set into a set of double doors at the end of the corridor, a conference room that was lit and occupied.

She stopped.

The conference room held perhaps twelve people around a long table. At the far end, a screen showed a projection she could not read from this distance — data, charts, something dense. A young man in a signals uniform was standing at the screen, a laser pointer in his hand, speaking. He had his back mostly to her.

She had not been in a Ministry briefing room before, not one like this. She had been in conference rooms, standard ones. This was different — not in its furniture, which was the same grey-laminate-and-ergonomic-chair configuration as every other room, but in the quality of the stillness in it. The twelve people around the table were very still. The kind of still that meant they were paying the most focused attention they were capable of.

She watched for a moment. She could not hear anything through the glass.

---

The young man at the screen advanced the slide. A new chart appeared — she could see the shape of it from here, two vertical bars of different heights, a horizontal axis. The young man pointed at the shorter bar. He said something. He pointed at the taller bar. He said something else.

The twelve people at the table did not move.

At the head of the table, a figure who had his back to the corridor. Older, from what she could see of him — the set of his shoulders, the particular stillness of a man who had stopped needing to demonstrate that he was listening. He was not looking at the screen. He was looking at his hands, which were flat on the table in front of him.

The young man finished his presentation. He stepped back. He said something that looked, from the movement of his mouth, like a single short sentence.

The figure at the head of the table did not move for a moment. Then he nodded.

---

She understood that she was watching a decision being made. She did not know what decision. She did not know the people in the room, or the charts on the screen, or the subject of the briefing. She knew the building — the Ministry of Economic Affairs, third floor, a corridor she had arrived on by mistake while trying to get to Procurement — and she knew the smell of the air and the grey carpet and the glass panel in the double doors.

She stood there for a moment longer.

The figure at the head of the table had not looked up. The young man in the signals uniform was gathering his materials. The twelve people around the table were beginning to move — the particular micro-movement of people in a meeting that has just ended, the small collective exhale, the reaching for phones and notebooks. Normal. Entirely normal.

She turned and walked back to the lift.

She pressed the button for the second floor. She submitted the contract amendment. She took the correct lift back to the lobby and walked out into the afternoon, which was hot and bright and smelled of exhaust and the noodle stall on the corner.

She did not think about the conference room again for two years.
