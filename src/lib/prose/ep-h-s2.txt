The offer had been extraordinary. That was the word everyone used — extraordinary — and it was accurate in both senses of the German *außergewöhnlich*: unusual and outside the ordinary. The new government in Beijing, presenting itself as a reformed Chinese administration pursuing peaceful reunification, had extended a package of trade terms to the European Union that no rational negotiating team could be expected to refuse.

Zero-tariff access for EU agricultural products — a market of 1.4 billion consumers that had been closed or heavily restricted for two decades. Technology transfer commitments in green energy and semiconductor manufacturing — the latter was particularly significant, because semiconductor manufacturing access had been the single most contested element of EU-China trade policy for five years. Infrastructure co-investment funds for twelve EU member states, each calibrated to that state's specific development priorities.

Each offer was tailored. Each offer was targeted. Each offer was, in the language of diplomatic assessment, *irresistible*.

The EU had accepted. The acceptance had been unanimous, which was itself extraordinary — the twenty-seven member states rarely agreed on anything related to China with less than three abstentions and a Danish reservation. The acceptance had been processed through the standard EU trade policy mechanisms. The legal documentation was clean. The political will was clear. The offer was too good to refuse, and so it had not been refused.

This was before the AU vote. Before the embargo. Before the drone strikes on Langata. Before the war that was now happening in East Africa was something anyone in Berlin had considered possible.

Now Brandt was sitting in her office with the text of the offer the EU had already accepted, and she was looking at a detail that should not have been in it.
