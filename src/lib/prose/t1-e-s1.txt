James Huang signed the last page at 2:47pm on a Thursday in March.

The handover documentation ran to four hundred and twelve pages. He had checked every page. He had walked through every room in the facility — the server halls, the cooling plant, the backup generation building, the lower level with the locked door he had never been given the key to, the corridor with the conduit he had not asked about. Everything was correct. Everything was exactly what he had built. The building was his work, and his work was done.

The facilities coordinator — a woman named Liu who worked for the Ministry and who had been on-site for the past six months handling the transition — was standing at the door of the server room with a clipboard and two signature pages. James signed the first one: facility handover, structural and systems, certified complete. He signed the second: equipment handover, received by operational authority.

The operational authority was waiting by the door.

He was young. Twenty-eight, maybe twenty-nine. He was wearing a signals division uniform — the particular dark green of Taiwanese military intelligence, the insignia that indicated a rank James did not know how to read. He was holding an access card and a tablet and a duffel bag. He looked like someone who had been travelling. He looked tired in the specific way that people looked tired when they had been working on something they were not allowed to talk about and had been doing it for a long time.
