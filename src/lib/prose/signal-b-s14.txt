Wu Shao-han reached the end of the document.

He sat for a moment looking at the closing seal placeholder — a grey rectangle where the system had indicated a visual element that had not yet been rendered, labelled APPENDIX 1 in brackets.

He scrolled back to the top.

He read it again, more slowly this time, though he was not sure what he was looking for. He had understood it the first time. He had understood every word. He had understood the structure — he had studied classical Chinese history at university, before the intelligence service, and the structure was not unfamiliar to him. He knew what a Mandate declaration looked like. He had read three of them in a seminar on dynastic transition.

He had never read one that was written in the future tense.

He sat for a moment longer. The server room hummed. Somewhere in the facility, a cooling unit cycled. The terminal cursor blinked at the bottom of the final page.

He thought about his grandfather, who had come to Taiwan in 1949 on a military transport with a single case of belongings and the understanding that he would return to Fujian within two years once the mainland was retaken. His grandfather had died in Taipei in 2008 at the age of eighty-nine. He had never been back. He had never stopped believing he would go back.

Wu Shao-han had grown up with that story. He had grown up knowing that the constitutional claim was not a fiction — it was a wound that had never closed, that had been packed with gauze and left, that everyone had agreed not to press on because pressing on it hurt and there was nothing to be done about it anyway.

There was now something to be done about it.

He looked at the two numbers for a long time.

87.3%.

23.7%.
